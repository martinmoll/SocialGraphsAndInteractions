How to read the network

The two explorer tabs show the same analysis on two research fields. This page explains what each dot, colour and number means, and which question it answers.

Numbers on this page come from the run of 11 November 2026. Prototype: nothing here is checked by hand yet.

The question

Which topics connect citation communities that rarely cite each other, and do these topics differ from the topics inside the communities?

A research field is not one conversation. Papers cite inside their own group far more than outside it. A few papers have citation links into several groups. We call them bridges. We want to know what the bridges write about: the core topic of one group, or something that several groups share, such as a method or a common problem.

To answer this we need two things on the same papers: a network (who cites whom) and text (what each paper is about). Every measure below belongs to one of these two halves, or joins them.

The network half

Degree

How many papers in the sample is this paper linked to?

What it is
The number of edges at a node: papers it cites plus papers that cite it, inside the sample.
In the explorer
"links" in the selected-paper panel. The hub tag marks the 5% highest degrees.
Why it matters
A paper with degree 2 can have its two edges in two communities by chance. We only judge bridging for papers with degree ≥ 5.
What it misses
It is not the paper's total citation count. That count is shown separately as "cited N× overall".

Communities and modularity

Does the field split into groups of papers that mostly cite each other?

What it is
A community is a set of nodes with many edges inside and few edges out. The Louvain algorithm searches for the partition with the highest modularity. Modularity compares the share of edges inside communities with the share expected if edges were placed at random with the same degrees. 0 means no more grouped than random; values near 1 mean strongly grouped.
In the explorer
Dot colours and the legend at the bottom left. Each community is labelled with its most typical words. Click a legend row to isolate one community.
Numbers
Energy systems: 13 communities, modularity 0.62. Ocean science: 19 communities, modularity 0.70.
What it misses
Modularity says how grouped a partition is, not how correct it is. The algorithm always returns communities, also in a random network.

The null model

Is this structure more than what degree and publication year alone would produce?

What it is
A degree-preserving rewiring. We swap the targets of two citation edges, many times. Each paper keeps its number of references and its number of citations. A swap is allowed only when each new edge still points from a newer paper to an older one, because a paper cannot cite the future. We build 100 such networks and run the same analysis on each.
In the explorer
"modularity, 100 rewired nulls" in the headline numbers, and the grey bands in the topic chart.
Numbers
Modularity in the rewired networks: 0.29 in both fields, against 0.62 and 0.70 in the real ones. The communities are real structure.
What it misses
The rewiring removes all topical structure at once, so it is an easy opponent to beat.

Stability

Does the algorithm find the same communities when we run it again?

What it is
Louvain has a random element. We run it with 10 random seeds and compare each pair of partitions with NMI (explained below). 1 means identical.
Numbers
Energy systems 0.70, ocean science 0.77.
Why it matters
The communities are similar between runs, not identical. A paper near a border can be a bridge in one run and not in another. Treat a single bridge with care; trust patterns over many bridges more.

Participation coefficient and bridges

Does this paper connect several communities, or does it stay in its own?

What it is
For one node: take the share of its edges that go to each community, square the shares, add them, and subtract the sum from 1. If all edges go to one community the value is 0. If the edges spread evenly over many communities the value moves towards 1.
In the explorer
Dot size. Click a paper and read "Where its citation links go": that bar chart is the paper's degree split by community, which is what the coefficient summarises in one number.
Bridge
We call a paper a bridge when its coefficient is in the top 10% among papers with degree ≥ 5. Energy systems: 155 bridges. Ocean science: 202 bridges.
What it misses
A review paper cites widely by design, so it scores high without connecting ideas in any deeper way. The review-like title tag flags these. In energy systems 39% of bridges are flagged; in ocean science 3%.

Betweenness centrality

Does this paper sit on many of the shortest paths between other papers?

What it is
The share of shortest paths between pairs of nodes that pass through this node. We estimate it from 500 sampled start nodes.
In the explorer
"betweenness" in the selected-paper panel.
How we use it
As a second opinion only. It does not decide which papers are bridges. A paper with high participation and high betweenness is a stronger bridge candidate than one with high participation alone.

Coupling edges

Do two papers build on the same literature, also when they do not cite each other?

What it is
Bibliographic coupling: we draw an edge between two papers when their reference lists share at least 5 works.
In the explorer
Switch "edges" from citation to coupling. The dots stay in place and only the lines change.
What it misses
The threshold of 5 is our choice. No measure on this page uses the coupling graph yet; it is there to look at.

The text half

Text topics

What is each paper about, judged from its words alone?

What it is
Each paper is a bag of words from its title and abstract, with stop words removed. LDA (a topic model) finds 12 groups of words that tend to occur together, and gives each paper a share of each topic. We assign each paper to its largest topic.
In the explorer
"text topic" in the selected-paper panel, the topic filter, and "colour: text topic". A topic is shown by its top words, for example "T5 phytoplankton · nutrient · production · primary".
What it misses
Nobody has read the papers and named the topics yet; the labels are top words only. The topics change when we change the stop words or the number of topics. Papers without an abstract are assigned from the title alone: 57% of energy systems, 23% of ocean science.

Community labels (TF-IDF)

Which words are typical for this community and rare elsewhere?

What it is
TF-IDF gives a word a high weight when it is frequent in a text and rare in the collection. We add the weights over all papers of a community and show the top words.
In the explorer
The legend, for example "C4 hydrogen · production · storage · future".
What it misses
The words describe the community; they did not create it. Communities come from citations only.

Joining the two halves

NMI (normalised mutual information)

Do the citation communities and the text topics sort the papers into the same groups?

What it is
A number between 0 and 1 that compares two partitions of the same nodes. 0 means that knowing a paper's group in one partition tells nothing about its group in the other. 1 means the partitions are identical.
In the explorer
The table under the headline numbers. To see it, switch "colour" from citation community to text topic: if the two agreed, each blob would keep one clean colour.
Numbers
Citation communities against LDA topics: 0.29 (energy systems), 0.34 (ocean science). With labels shuffled: 0.01.
What it answers
Citations and text agree partly and far from fully. This gap is what makes the project possible: if the two were identical, the text could tell us nothing new about the bridges.

Topic share on bridges, and its z-score

Do bridges write about different things than a paper picked without regard to topic?

What it is
For each topic we take its share among the real bridges. We then find the bridges in each of the 100 rewired networks and take the topic's share there. The z-score is the real share minus the mean of the 100 null shares, divided by their standard deviation.
In the explorer
The chart "Topic share on bridges vs. null". Orange bar: real share. Grey band: null mean ± 2 standard deviations. A second, grey z-score repeats the test without review-like papers.
How to read z
Near 0: bridges carry this topic as often as chance. Above about +2 or below about −2: worth a look. With 12 topics tested, one value near ±2 can appear by chance alone.
Numbers
Energy systems: smart grid +7.0, storage +3.6, hybrid system optimisation −6.3. Ocean science: no topic beyond about ±2.3.
What it answers
This is the main result. In energy systems the bridges carry shared problems such as storage and grids, and not the core optimisation topic. In ocean science the bridges write about the same things as the rest of the field.

Missing links

Which papers write about the same thing and do not see each other in citations?

What it is
Pairs of papers with high cosine similarity between their TF-IDF vectors, in different communities, and at least 3 citation steps apart (shortest path length ≥ 3).
In the explorer
The "Missing links" list at the bottom of the right panel. Click either paper of a pair.
What it misses
The similarity counts shared words, not shared meaning. No pair is checked by hand yet.

A route through the explorer

  1. Look at the communities. Read the legend. Click a row to isolate one community, and click again to release it.
  2. Compare with the text. Switch "colour" to text topic. See how much the colours mix inside each blob.
  3. Find the bridges. Tick "bridges only" and see where in the picture they sit.
  4. Open one bridge. Click a paper in "Top 20 bridge papers". Read where its links go, and check the review and hub tags.
  5. Read the topic chart. Find the topics with a z-score beyond ±2. Click a row to see where that topic sits in the network.
  6. Switch to coupling edges. See which communities draw on the same literature.

Controls: scroll to zoom, drag to move, hover for a title, click for details. Type a title word in the search box and press Enter to jump to the first match.

What you should not conclude

  • Position is not data. The layout is drawn for the eye.
  • A bridge is not automatically important. It has links into many communities. A review has that by design.
  • A topic label is not a finding. The labels are the model's top words.
  • The two fields are not directly comparable yet. One sample is defined by a phrase, the other by a subfield.
  • The count of bridges proves nothing against the null. A rewired network has weak communities, so almost every paper in it looks like a bridge. The test is on the topics, not on the count.
