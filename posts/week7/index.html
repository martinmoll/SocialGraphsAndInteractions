One vector for Storm

Last week’s word vectors gave every bank the same numbers, river or money. GloVe does the same to Storm. Its one vector for storm sits next to hurricane (0.88) and winds, and x-men is down at 0.20. A static embedding can hold one sense per word, and for this word the sense is weather.

A contextual encoder computes a fresh vector each time the word appears. We took four sentences from Storm’s own page and four weather reports we wrote, and pulled BERT’s vector for storm out of each. Her sentences agree with each other (0.63), the weather sentences agree with each other (0.73), and across the two groups the cosine falls to 0.28. All 170 sentences on her page that name her stay that far from the weather.

On Test 2

A static embedding gives a word type one fixed vector; a contextual representation depends on the surrounding words, so the two banks, or the two storms, get different vectors.

cosine similarity · course · weeks 5–6
\( \cos(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\dfrac{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert} \)

Static vectors aren’t blind to Marvel. GloVe lists symbiote among the neighbours of venom (0.58), because news and Wikipedia write about the comic often enough to leave a trace. The single vector is the limit: widow sits beside wife and niece, cable beside network, and none of those vectors can move when the sentence is about a superhero.

The name leaks into the page vector

The course atlas embeds whole pages with all-MiniLM-L6-v2. The encoder reads at most 256 word pieces, so each page is cut into passages of up to 180 words, every passage is embedded, and the page becomes the mean of its passages: 384 numbers. Then we changed one thing. On Black Widow’s page we replaced all 165 uses of her codename with Natasha Romanoff and embedded the page again.

Black Cat fell from her second-nearest page to the 19th. Black Marvel fell from 7th to 51st. Yelena Belova and the Spider-Women, who share her story, stayed at the top. It works in reverse too: Black Cat renamed Felicia Hardy loses Black Widow (1st to 12th), and Black Panther renamed T’Challa loses Black Cat (3rd to 20th). The encoder treats the word Black as something these pages are about.

Storm is the control. With Ororo Munroe in place of her codename, Emma Frost stays her nearest page and the X-Men stay close. The page that leaves is Human Torch, from 14th to 74th. His real name is Johnny Storm.

On Test 2

A document embedding is one dense vector per text. Nearest neighbours come from cosine in the original space; a 2D projection is a drawing of it, and its nearest dots can differ.

a page as a vector · course · week 7
\( \mathbf{x}_i = [x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{384}] \)

None of this shows on a map. PCA’s two axes keep 9.9% of the variance of these vectors, so the nearest dots on a 2D drawing are a poor guide to the nearest pages. Every rank above comes from the original 384 dimensions.

Names read as feelings

A sentiment tool doesn’t know who Doom is. It sees a word. The VADER lexicon has human ratings for doom (-1.7) and ghost (-1.3), so Ghost Rider and Doom start below zero before a verb arrives, and Captain Marvel starts above it because marvel is rated +1.8.

We put 19 codenames from the corpus into three plain sentences and asked DistilBERT, the movie-review classifier from the course page. “Abomination arrived in New York” comes back negative at 0.97. The same name in “Abomination was created by Stan Lee and Jack Kirby” comes back positive at 0.99. Seventeen of the names are positive in every frame; Storm and Black Widow score 1.00.

Learned classifiers pick up names too. The TF-IDF and logistic-regression model we trained on news headlines in exercise 7.6 has trump as its most negative feature, with a weight of -1.73. The sign says the word pushes a headline toward the negative class. It doesn’t say whether the headlines were negative because of him or the coverage was.

On Test 2

Lexicon score of “Ghost Rider saved the city from Doom” with VADER’s ratings: ghost −1.3 + saved +1.8 + doom −1.7 = -1.2. A lookup scores words, so names, negation and sarcasm all slip through.

On Test 2

A confidence of 0.99 says the model is sure. It isn’t evidence that the score measures sentiment in a new domain: this model was fine-tuned on movie reviews.

The tagger knows people by their real names

Named-entity recognition gives every token a tag. In BIO notation B begins a span, I continues it and O is outside. spaCy’s small English model reads our own sentence below. Venom is a place, like New York, and Spider-Man is an organisation. The model learned from news, where codenames are rare and Venom looks like the name of a town.

Across all 33,752 sentences of the corpus the pattern holds. Steve Rogers is a person in 105 of 106 mentions, Peter Parker in 222 of 225, Tony Stark in 78 of 79. Their codenames lose the person. Spider-Man is a person in 601 of 1,888 mentions and Iron Man in 134 of 395. Captain America is a person once in 615: his usual tag is a place (377 times), because the tagger reads America. Venom is a place in 547 of 643, and Doctor Strange is never tagged at all. The button below, like the switch in the top bar, gives every name sticker on this page the tag spaCy most often gives that name.

We checked the model before trusting this. On 40 sentences we labelled by hand in exercise 7.8 it found 15% of hero names and 70% of personal names. Finding a span is also only half the job. To a reader, Steve Rogers and Captain America are one man; to the tagger they are two strings. Deciding they belong together is resolution, and no tagger does it for you.

On Test 2

Read and write BIO tags: B begins an entity span, I continues it, O marks a token outside one. Extraction finds the spans; resolution decides that Steve Rogers and Captain America are the same entity.

span precision and recall · adapted · exercise 7.8
\( P=\dfrac{\text{correct spans}}{\text{model spans}} \)
\( R=\dfrac{\text{correct spans}}{\text{reference spans}} \)

So the tagger misses Captain America 614 times out of 615. A network built from its output loses him too, and the network from weeks 1 to 4 never had him. He has no page.

Captain America, everywhere and nowhere

Three of Marvel’s best-known heroes have no page among our 303: Captain America, Thor and Iron Man. The corpus has Iron Man (Ultimate Marvel character) and Iron Man 2020, who are other men. All three are still all over the text. Captain America is named on 115 pages, Iron Man on 99 and Thor on 63.

For characters who do have a page, being named and being linked go together. Across the 267 characters whose name belongs to no one else, the number of other pages naming them and their hyperlink in-degree correlate at ρ = 0.82. By that measure only Spider-Man (142 pages) is more central than Captain America, and Thor comes after Spider-Man and Wolverine.1

On Test 2

An entity co-occurrence network joins two entities when they appear in the same unit of text. Changing the unit from sentence to paragraph changes what an edge claims, so it is a modelling decision.

On Test 2

A count of entities is only as good as the resolution behind it. “Captain America” on 115 pages is not 115 pages about one man.

A count of 115 pages still overstates him. We read 40 random mentions. 20 are the character. 12 are titles: issue numbers like Captain America #153, a series, a film. 8 are the mantle on someone else: Bucky after Steve Rogers’s death, Sam Wilson, William Burnside, Jeffrey Mace, William Naslund in the 1940s. The name is a job, and several of the men who held it have pages here.

Pick a square to read the mention. Filled: the character · yellow: a title · dashed: someone else in the role.

Resolution decides which of those mentions become the node. Map Steve Rogers to Captain America and he enters the co-mention network built from spaCy’s person spans at rank 17 by degree when an edge means two people in one sentence, and rank 24 when it means one paragraph. Same spans, two networks (13,386 and 37,278 edges). The corpus carries the opposite trap as well. The Ultimate Iron Man page has 1 incoming link, while 98 pages say Iron Man about someone else. Match names to nodes without resolution and that page inherits the hero.

Thor walks in

A missing page can be added. We took the current Wikipedia articles for the three heroes and passed them through the same encoder. Nothing was retrained: the model learned its weights long before it saw Marvel, and only three new vectors were computed. It places them well. Thor’s nearest pages are Dargo Ktor and Beta Ray Bill, two other bearers of the hammer, then Thor Girl and Balder. Captain America’s are William Burnside, Nomad and Bucky, men who wore his name.

The maps have a harder time. PCA is a fixed projection, so it can drop a new page onto the old map, and it puts Thor beside Hulk and The Witness, neither of them anywhere near his list of nearest pages. t-SNE has no way to add a point. It redraws, Thor lands among the Asgardians, and the 303 old pages keep only 56% of their five nearest dots, although not one of their cosine neighbours changed.

On Test 2

A pretrained encoder was trained on another corpus; passing your documents through it only computes their vectors. t-SNE keeps local neighbourhoods and makes global distances unreliable.

On Test 2

Three ways to group the same pages: an LDA topic (shared word counts), an embedding cluster (nearby dense vectors) and a network community (hyperlinks). They don’t have to agree.

BERTopic clusters the same page vectors and calls Thor and Iron Man outliers: neither is dense enough to join a cluster of our 303. It files Captain America with Bucky, U.S. Agent and Union Jack, the topic of patriotic and wartime heroes. That makes three groupings of the same pages, LDA by shared words in week 6, BERTopic by nearby vectors, Louvain by hyperlinks, and each answers a different question.

Ask the corpus for Thor

Search is where a question meets a corpus that may not hold the answer. We asked for “the Norse god of thunder”. TF-IDF matches the words and returns Beta Ray Bill. The dense retriever, which scores each page by its best 60-word passage, returns Ultimate Iron Man at 0.68.

For the eight course queries whose page exists, the right page scores between 0.28 and 0.64. The missing answer scored higher than every right one. A similarity score says how close the nearest passage is, and nothing about whether the corpus holds an answer at all.

Hand that passage to a language model and it would answer from Ultimate Iron Man. That’s a retrieval error: the generator never had the right evidence. Had the passage been right and the answer still wrong, the error would belong to generation, and keeping the retrieved passage visible is how you tell the two apart. Once Thor’s page exists, TF-IDF ranks it first and the dense retriever second.

On Test 2

Sparse retrieval (TF-IDF) rewards shared words; dense retrieval compares meaning and can bridge vocabulary mismatch. “A deaf archer who never misses”: TF-IDF finds Hawkeye first, the dense retriever ranks him 35th.

On Test 2

RAG has two stages. Retrieval picks passages, generation writes from them. Wrong passage: retrieval error. Right passage, wrong answer: generation error.

Wrong Storm, missing Thor.

The codenames did the damage. Every tool that reads words one at a time, a static vector, a lexicon, a tagger trained on news, took a superhero’s name for the ordinary word inside it. The tools that read context did better, and still answered with full confidence when the answer wasn’t in the corpus. We only saw each of these failures by reading the text behind a number, the habit the course has asked for since week 1.

Limits. Every number is one run of each model on one corpus. The weather sentences, the sentence frames, the alias table and the reading of 40 mentions are our own choices. The three newcomer articles are today’s versions; they changed size by less than 0.3% since the corpus snapshot of 26 August.

The course’s Dark Matter explorables score a Marvel fan-fiction novel sentence by sentence. Their sharpest disagreement is about a name: “An eerie stillness comes from the King of Wakanda…” gets +0.96 from the movie-review classifier and −0.96 from VADER, and DeepMoji reads it as 👿 😡 😠. Across the novel the two instruments agree on the sign of only 36% of sentences. An emotional arc is the shape of the smoothed scores, and the level drops out, which is how two instruments can disagree on most sentences and still draw arcs that rise and fall together.

For unit vectors it equals the dot product, so ranking by either gives the same order.

TF-IDFcourse · week 6
\( \mathrm{tfidf}(t,d)=\dfrac{\mathrm{count}(t,d)}{|d|}\,\log\dfrac{N}{\mathrm{df}(t)} \)

The sparse retriever’s weights.

lexicon scoreadapted · week 7
\( \mathrm{score}(d)=\sum_{w\in d} s(w) \)

The course describes it in words: give words scores, find them, add them up. s(w) is a word’s rating.

\( P(\text{positive}\mid\mathbf{x})=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-(b+\sum_j \beta_j x_j)}} \)
