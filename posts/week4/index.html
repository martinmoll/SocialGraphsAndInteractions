01A map of philosophy

Every philosopher born before 1900 on Wikipedia's lists, 1,374 of them in one connected piece, joined by 9,139 links. A link means one article links to the other; its weight counts how many times, summed over both directions. 5,899 links are a single mention. The heaviest, Erasmus – Thomas More, has 24.

The map below shows the traditions of philosophy as regions: antiquity, the scholastics, the Germans, the Enlightenment, Indian and Chinese thought. It is a skeleton of the network, and section 7 explains how it was drawn and what it leaves out. Everything in between is measured on the full network.

Colour: Louvain communities of the full network, each named after its highest-degree member. The names are our labels.
Size: strength, the total weight of a philosopher's links, \(s_i = \sum_j w_{ij}\). Aristotle leads with 521 over 300 links.

02Are there traditions at all?

A community is a group of nodes more densely linked among themselves than to the rest. Louvain finds one by climbing modularity: the fraction of links inside communities, minus the fraction you would expect if the links were shuffled with everyone's degree kept. It finds 9 traditions with \(Q = 0.503\).

Is that a lot? A random network has communities too, because an optimiser always finds some borders that cross fewer links than expected. The same algorithm on 20 shuffled copies of the network finds \(Q = 0.222 \pm 0.002\). Our traditions are 136 standard deviations above that. They are real.

\(Q = \sum_c \left[\frac{\ell_c}{m} - \left(\frac{d_c}{2m}\right)^2\right]\), with \(\ell_c\) the links inside community \(c\) and \(d_c\) its summed degree.
Newman's greedy merging, the older method, finds 15 groups with \(Q = 0.416\). A merge is never undone, so early merges snowball into blobs of 550, 481, 262.
On the testModularity scores how grouped a partition is, not how right. Report it against its null, as a difference or a z-score.

03Where do the traditions meet?

Girvan and Newman's idea: a link between two groups carries every shortest path between them, so it has a high edge betweenness. Their algorithm cuts the busiest link, recomputes and repeats. One pass takes about 4 seconds here, so a full run would take about 10 hours. One pass is enough for our question.

The busiest links do cross between traditions: 18 of the top 20, against 35% of all links. And 16 of those 20 are a single passing mention. The busiest of all is Ramanuja – Thomas Aquinas, weight 1.

Now count a strong tie as a short step, length \(1/w\). The busiest roads become strong ties, and the one between Chinese philosophy and the rest is Confucius – Voltaire, weight 3, ranked #3 of 9,139. Keep that link in mind.

Edge betweenness: the number of shortest paths that run along a link. Girvan–Newman costs \(O(m^2 n)\): one full recomputation per removed link.
Weighted distance: link length \(1/w_{ij}\), shortest paths by Dijkstra's algorithm instead of breadth-first search.
On the testGirvan–Newman recomputes after every cut because a removal reroutes the traffic onto the remaining bridges.

04How sure are we of the borders?

Louvain visits philosophers in a random order, so another seed draws slightly different borders. Across 30 seeds it finds 7 to 10 traditions, and any two runs agree at a normalized mutual information of 0.61 to 0.85.

The large picture holds and the borders move, and the same philosophers keep moving. A typical philosopher keeps 65% of their community-mates from run to run. The least settled are the Scientific Revolution and the thinkers around it: Alexandre Koyré, Emanuel Swedenborg, Paracelsus, Galileo Galilei, Lucretius, Marin Mersenne keep about a quarter. Section 3 holds in every run: 17–18 of the busiest 20 links still cross.

Normalized mutual information: 1 for identical partitions, 0 for independent ones. Use it against ground truth when you have it and between runs when you do not.
Kept community-mates: the Jaccard overlap of a philosopher's community in two runs, averaged over all pairs of runs. It needs no matching of labels.
On the testTwo runs with nearly equal Q can still split the network differently. A partition is one sample, not the truth.

05What are the meeting links made of?

If traditions are built from repeated mentions and joined by passing ones, links across borders should be lighter. They are. 35% of links cross between traditions but carry 30% of the weight. 73% of them are a single mention, against 60% inside a tradition. It holds in every run: mean weight 1.45–1.54 across, 1.81–1.86 inside.

Strong ties build the traditions, and weak ties join them.

Weighted modularity uses \(w_{ij}\) for \(A_{ij}\), strengths for degrees and the total weight for \(m\). Weighted Louvain scores 0.55, higher because strong ties sit inside groups.
On the testA weighted modularity is not comparable to the unweighted one: they answer different questions.

06Who stands where traditions meet?

A partition must give each philosopher exactly one tradition. For Aristotle that is a problem. Only 103 of his 300 links stay inside his own group, and 74 go to the scholastics, who called him simply the Philosopher. Across 30 runs he sits with Plato 16 times and with Aquinas 13 times.

Overlapping methods let him keep both. Clique percolation at \(k = 5\) puts him in 6 communities, more than anyone. Link communities, which cluster links instead of philosophers, put him in 23.

\(k\)-clique community: two \(k\)-cliques are adjacent when they share \(k-1\) nodes; a community is every clique reachable by rolling from one to the next.
Link community: links are similar when their end nodes' neighbourhoods overlap; a node inherits all its links' communities. 693 philosophers are in two or more.
On the testAn overlapping node belongs to several groups, and a partition must get all but one of them wrong.

07Can a drawing show the meeting points?

Drawn whole, 9,139 links are a hairball. A global threshold keeps heavy links only, but a weight of 2 is nothing to Aristotle and the strongest tie a minor commentator has. The disparity filter asks each philosopher separately: is this link a surprisingly large share of your strength? It keeps the link if either end says yes.

\((1 - p_{ij})^{k_i - 1} < \alpha, \quad p_{ij} = w_{ij} / s_i\)

At similar link counts the two keep different people: 112 philosophers stay attached only under the filter, 25 only under the threshold. But neither keeps the busiest roads of section 3. A weight-1 link cannot pass the test below \(\alpha = 0.381\), so the ties that join traditions are exactly what a backbone removes.

09What holds philosophy together?

Strong ties build the traditions; weak ties join them. The traditions are real, far above their null. The links between them are lighter, and the busiest ones are single mentions.

The busiest roads are mostly analogies. Ramanuja – Thomas Aquinas carries the most shortest paths and records a modern comparison. Counting strength instead finds Confucius – Voltaire, which records real influence.

The borders move, and the same people sit on them: the Scientific Revolution, and Aristotle.

No backbone keeps the weak ties. A skeleton shows the traditions clearly and hides how they meet. So we drew the busiest roads back on top, and said what was removed.

Choices we made

  • Louvain seed 0, unweighted; traditions named after their highest-degree member.
  • 20 degree-preserving shuffles as the null for \(Q\), the only thing borrowed from earlier weeks.
  • One edge-betweenness pass by hops, checked with \(1/w\) lengths; no full Girvan–Newman run.
  • \(k = 5\) for clique percolation; link communities cut at the maximum partition density.
  • \(\alpha = 0.3\) for the drawing, with the dial for every other value.

What we cannot conclude

  • That the traditions are the true schools. The names are ours, and the partition only partly matches the century lists.
  • That a link is an influence. Section 8 shows it can be a comparison.
  • That the busiest roads mattered historically. Shortest paths are a property of how Wikipedia's articles link.
On the testThe five drawing rules: backbone first, with a method and parameter you can defend and what was removed; a layout of the backbone; colour by communities found on the full data; size by strength; label the important nodes.
