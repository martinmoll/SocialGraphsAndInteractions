Week 6 gave us several ways to turn text into vectors and compare them. We put the same question to all of them, asking which of the 303 Marvel characters are mutants, and scored every answer against Wikipedia's own category. The answers disagree by a factor of twenty, and the reasons they disagree cover most of what the week teaches.

the yardstickHow do we keep score?

A fair test needs an answer key that none of the tools wrote. Wikipedia keeps one: Category:Marvel Comics mutants. We asked which of our 303 titles sit directly in it. 49 do, 242 do not, and 12 titles now redirect to other articles, so they carry no label and stay out of every score below.1

For each tool we report two numbers. Precision is the share of the pages it calls mutants that are in the category; recall is the share of the 49 category members it finds. Neither is accuracy in the strict sense. The category is a label that editors wrote, and the last section shows it disagreeing with the pages themselves.

documents · tf-idf · scattertextDoes the page say so?

The bluntest test is to look for the word.2 It catches 47 of the 49 category members, a recall of 0.96. It also names 64 pages that are not in the category, so only 42% of its mutants are mutants by Wikipedia's count.

Reading those pages explains most of the noise. Deadpool says mutant 14 times and Bird-Brain 13, and nearly every use is the title of a comic, The New Mutants, where both of them first appeared. The Phoenix Force page says it 16 times because it retells X-Men stories; the Force itself is a cosmic entity.3

TF-IDF, the week's first tool, asks a sharper question: is this word typical of the page, or common everywhere? Mutant appears on 97 pages, so its weight per occurrence is moderate. Words found on every page, like marvel and comics, get an idf of 0. A name that appears on one page gets ln 303 = 5.71, and names fill these pages, so mutant makes the ten highest-weighted words of only 7 pages. Asking for five uses instead of one moves the trade the other way: precision 0.78, recall 0.63.

By handidf(mutant) = ln(303 / 97) = 1.139. The page with the highest tf-idf for it is Redneck (comics): 7 uses on a short page.

It sits on 39 of the X-Men pages and 10 of the cosmic ones.4 Still, the X-Men community holds only 30 of the 49 mutants. The rest are spread across the network.

topic models · ldaCan a model find them without the word?

LDA describes each page as a mixture of topics and each topic as a probability distribution over words. A page can lean on a "mutant" topic without using the word, as long as it uses enough of the topic's other words. We ran the course page's own LDA code, with its name filter, 60 times: K = 5, 8 and 12 topics, 20 random seeds each.

That leaves two decisions, and we took both from the week-6 page. The page names a topic "mutants" because its word list starts with mutant, so a run's mutant topic is the one headed by mutant or mutants.5 A page counts as a member when that topic holds more than half of its mixture θ. Exercise 6.4 contrasts a page of [0.70, 0.20, 0.10], which one label sums up, with [0.35, 0.35, 0.30], which it does not, and 0.5 is the lowest cut that separates the two.6

On Test 2In LDA a topic is a distribution over words and a document is a mixture over topics. Topic labels are human interpretations, and the number of topics, the preprocessing and the initialization can all change the fit.

Turn the seed and the answer moves. In 7 of the 20 runs at K = 5 (and 5 at K = 8, 3 at K = 12) no topic is headed by mutant. The word has merged into a topic about symbiotes, vampires, armour, magic or Wikipedia's media boilerplate, the "split, merge or disappear" the course page warns about. When a mutant topic exists, it holds anywhere from 3.1% to 42.7% of the corpus's tokens.7

Exercise 6.5 asks whether a topic is stable, and by its test this one mostly is. Matched one to one against seed 0,8 the mutant topic of a later seed is the same topic refitted in 10 of 12, 11 of 14 and 12 of 16 later runs that have one (K = 5, 8 and 12). What changes is its size. With the page's own settings (K = 8, seed 0) it claims 96 pages; seed 1 gives 45 and seed 2 gives 126. Its precision against the category ranges from 0.25 to 0.83 across runs, depending on a seed that a report would normally not even mention.

Quasar shows how the model gets there. His page never says mutant, yet he is a member in 10 of the 60 runs, with θ = 0.84 at the page's settings. His powers come from the Quantum Bands, and his page uses energy 15 times. In that run energy is the mutant topic's third word, carried in by the cosmic heroes the topic also absorbs. The model judges a page by the company of its words, and for Quasar the company points the wrong way.

Size, not identityThe same fit at θ above 0.3 or 0.7 would claim 129 or 62 pages instead of 96.

That raises a question about mutant itself: what company does the word keep? The second half of the week can answer it.

4vector spaces, one cosine, four different sets of neighbours for mutant

word-context · ppmi · word2vec · gloveWhich words keep mutant company?

So far a row was a page. Turn the matrix around and each row is a word, each column a context word, and each cell counts how often the two occur within a small window of each other. By the distributional hypothesis, words with similar rows tend to have related meanings, and cosine compares the rows.

Raw counts have the problem the week started with. The most frequent context of mutant is the, 141 times. PMI asks whether a pair occurs more often than independence would predict. For (mutant, the) it barely does, with PMI 0.075; (mutant, powers) occurs 30 times and scores 3.54. One oddity: mutant and mutants never occur within two words of each other, so that pair gets a PPMI of 0 although the two words mean the same thing.

One cell by hand(mutant, powers): P(w, c) = 30 / 1,286,291, P(w) = 1,659 / 1,286,291, P(c) = 2,005 / 1,286,291. The ratio is about 11.6, and log₂ of it is 3.54.

positive PMIcourse · week 6\( \mathrm{PPMI}(w,c)=\max(\mathrm{PMI}(w,c),0) \)

The same cosine, applied to four kinds of vector, returns four different neighbourhoods. Raw counts put supervillain, group, threat and gang closest, at 0.87 to 0.89. These words fill the same slot in a sentence, and their rows look alike mostly because the and a dominate every row: two random words already average 0.39.

PPMI finds associated words: mutants, powers, abilities, group, human. Its cosines look tiny, 0.12 to 0.16, against a random average of 0.03. As a rank, its top neighbour beats 99.9% of random pairs, the same rank that raw counts reach at 0.89. Word2Vec, trained on the pages with the course settings, finds Marvel lore: subspecies, omega, humans, magneto, supremacy, telepath, and all six stay in the top ten in at least four of five seeds. GloVe, trained on Wikipedia and news, finds the world outside the comics: mutated, ninja, werewolf, cyborg, mice. It has one vector for mutant, and that vector sits as close to mice (0.59) as to x-men (0.58).

Widen the PPMI window from two words to ten and the neighbours drift from associates toward topics: magneto, jean and men come in, along with later.11 The vectors and the window decide what a cosine measures, and its value can only be compared within its own space.

Word2Vec in two linesSkip-gram gives the model a centre word and trains it to recognize the words around it. Negative sampling contrasts each real pair with k random words (k = 5 here) so the model never scores the whole vocabulary.

PPMI counts and Word2Vec predicts, yet both put mutants among the nearest words to mutant. The week's Goodies explain why.

r 0.73between what Word2Vec learned and what PPMI counts, over 71,357 pairs

goodies · not on test 2Why do counting and predicting agree?

Word2Vec never builds a co-occurrence matrix. Skip-gram learns two vectors per word: w for when the word is in the centre and c for when it is a context. Training pushes w · c up for real pairs and down for random ones. Levy and Goldberg (2014), linked from the course Goodies, showed that at the optimum this makes w · c equal to the pair's PMI minus log k. gensim keeps the context vectors, so the claim can be checked on our pages.

For the 71,357 word-context pairs that occur at least ten times within five words, the learned w · c and the counted PMI − ln 5 correlate at r = 0.727 (0.728 on a second seed), and their averages are close: −1.08 against −0.90. The cloud follows the line up to a PMI − ln 5 of about 1 and then flattens below it. The strongest associations, mostly between rarer words, are the ones 100 dimensions cannot fully hold.12 Word2Vec has learned a compressed copy of the PMI matrix, and that is why its neighbours and PPMI's so often agree.

Mutant's own pairsFor the 55 contexts of mutant, r = 0.78. The highest PMI belongs to population, nation, anti.

Who counts as a mutant? 49 characters, say Wikipedia's editors, and two of them are never called one on their own pages.

The word reads book titles, the topic model reads energy, and each vector space has its own idea of close. The cosine was the same in every section; what changed was what we asked it to compare. This post has limits of its own: the key is a label someone wrote, one corpus is a small sample of language, and both of our LDA rules are readings of the course page that someone else could make differently.
