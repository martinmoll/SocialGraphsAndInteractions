Week 2 · Models & null models · 277 Marvel characters

The paradox is in the degrees, the triangles are in the wiring

Week 2 built networks from simple recipes and asked what each recipe gets right. We lined three classic models up against Marvel, then shuffled Marvel itself to find out which of its properties are just its degrees.

In short
  • 8.6×Marvel is a small world. It is 8.6 times as clustered as a random network, at the same distance.
  • 0 of 3No classic model gets everything. All three get the short paths; only Watts–Strogatz gets the clustering, only Barabási–Albert the hubs.
  • 16×Your random friend is a hub. Spider-Man turns up as a random friend 16 times as often as his fair share.
  • 58%of Marvel's extra clustering is in the wiring. The friendship paradox is entirely in the degrees.

What we asked

Network science started with a loop: propose a simple mechanism, build a network with it, compare it to a real one, and let the mismatch show which ingredient is missing. The three classic models are that loop run three times. Then comes a twist, the null model: what would the network look like if nothing interesting were going on?

So we asked: which of Marvel's properties can each model reproduce? And which survive a shuffle that keeps every character's number of links but scrambles who they link to?

What we measured

Every comparison network has Marvel's \(n = 277\) characters and about its \(m = 1{,}421\) links, so they share the same average degree. What differs is how the links are arranged. Four numbers capture that:

\(P(k)\)Degree distribution: the share of characters with \(k\) links. Its spread decides whether there are hubs, so we also report the biggest one.
\(\langle d\rangle\)Average distance: the mean number of hops along the shortest path between two characters.
\(C_i = \dfrac{2e_i}{k_i(k_i-1)}\)Local clustering: \(e_i\) counts the linked pairs among the friends of \(i\), out of \(k_i(k_i-1)/2\) possible (\(C_i = 0\) below two friends). Spider-Man: 462 of 5,565, \(C_i = 0.08\). Average clustering \(C\) is the mean over everyone.
\(\langle k_{\text{friend}}\rangle \,/\, \langle k\rangle\)Friendship paradox: how many times as many links a random character's friend has. More on this below.
Model 1 · Erdős–Rényi random network

Chance gets the distance right, and nothing else

The simplest recipe, \(G(n,p)\): link each of the \(n(n-1)/2\) possible pairs independently with the same probability \(p\). Everything follows from \(p\). We simulate its twin \(G(n,m)\), which places exactly \(m\) links at random and makes the same predictions.

\(\langle m\rangle = p\,\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2} \qquad \langle k\rangle = p\,(n-1)\)Links and average degree. Solve \(\langle m\rangle = m\) for \(p\) and you get the density: \(p = 0.037\) for our 277 characters (0.031 for all 303), so \(\langle k\rangle = 10.3\).
\(C = p \qquad \langle d\rangle \approx \dfrac{\ln n}{\ln \langle k\rangle}\)Clustering and distance. Your friends are linked with the same \(p\) as anyone. Each step multiplies your reach by \(\langle k\rangle\), and \(\langle k\rangle^d \approx n\) after \(d \approx \ln n/\ln\langle k\rangle\) steps. For Marvel: \(C = 0.037\), \(\langle d\rangle \approx 2.42\).
\(\begin{aligned} P(k) &= \dbinom{n-1}{k}\,p^k(1-p)^{n-1-k} \\ &\approx e^{-\langle k\rangle}\dfrac{\langle k\rangle^k}{k!} \end{aligned}\)Degree distribution. Binomial: \(k\) heads in \(n-1\) biased coin flips. In large sparse networks it becomes Poisson, peaked at \(\langle k\rangle\) with width \(\sigma = \sqrt{p(1-p)(n-1)} \approx \sqrt{\langle k\rangle} = 3.2\). 95% of characters would have 5–17 links, so there are no hubs.

Against Marvel, the distance is spot on: 2.66 hops against Marvel's 2.67. Everything else misses. Marvel's degrees spread \(\sigma = 11.1\), not 3.2, so Spider-Man has 106 links where the random network's biggest hub has 20. And Marvel is 8.6× as clustered (0.320 against 0.037).

Clustering far above chance, at chance-level distances, is exactly what a small world is. Marvel is one; the random network is not.

Model 2 · Watts–Strogatz small world

A few shortcuts make a clustered world small

Watts and Strogatz started at the opposite extreme: a ring lattice where every character links to their 10 nearest neighbours. Neighbours of neighbours are neighbours, so clustering is high, but paths crawl around the ring. Then each link is rewired to a random target with probability \(q\), a dial from order (\(q = 0\), the lattice) to randomness (\(q = 1\), a random network).

Shortcuts collapse the distance long before they dent the triangles: at \(q = 0.01\) the average distance drops from 14 to 6 hops, with clustering barely touched. The course's small-world range is \(q \approx 0.001\)–\(0.1\), but there clustering stays at 0.49 or more, far above Marvel's. So we use \(q = 0.2\), which makes this model's clustering fitted, not predicted.

What it can't make is hubs. Everyone starts with exactly 10 links and rewiring barely spreads them, so the biggest hub has 14.

Model 3 · Barabási–Albert preferential attachment

Growth plus popularity makes hubs

Barabási and Albert added the two ingredients the first models lack. Real networks grow, and newcomers prefer the popular. Characters arrive one at a time, each bringing 5 links, and attach to an existing character \(i\) with probability \(\Pi(k_i)\):

\(\Pi(k_i) = \dfrac{k_i}{\sum_j k_j}\)Preferential attachment. Every link a character gets makes the next one more likely. The rich get richer, and early leads snowball.
\(P(k) \sim k^{-\gamma},\; \gamma = 3\)A power law. Growth and preference together produce it; neither alone does. There is no typical degree, and on log–log axes it is a straight line.

The random network and Watts–Strogatz die out by 25 links. Only Barabási–Albert keeps up with Marvel's tail (biggest hub 65 ± 7). It misses the rest of Marvel, though. Every newcomer arrives with 5 links, so only 0.1% of its characters have fewer, against 33% of Marvel's. And its clustering is 0.099, 31% of Marvel's: links aimed at popular characters build stars, not triangles.

A side effect of hubs

Your random friend is probably a hub

Pick a random character, then one of their friends: the friend usually has more links. It isn't bad luck. A character with \(k\) links is somebody's friend \(k\) times, so hubs get picked over and over. How strong the effect is depends on how unequal the degrees are:

\(\langle k_{\text{friend}}\rangle \approx \dfrac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle} = \langle k\rangle + \dfrac{\sigma^2}{\langle k\rangle}\)Friendship paradox. \(\sigma^2\) is the variance of the degrees. A Poisson network has \(\sigma^2 = \langle k\rangle\), so friends get about one extra link: a ratio of 1.09. Marvel's \(\sigma^2 = 123\) gives 22.3. Picking friends directly gives 24.9, 2.43× a character's 10.3. The formula is exact for a random end of a random link; picking the person first gives a little more here.

Spider-Man alone is 5.8% of all friend picks, and the top ten characters are 22%. He is also the only character with no friend who has more links. (Across all 303, Radian would join him: he tops the nine-character island we left out.)

The null model

Shuffle Marvel and half the triangles disappear

So far a random network has been our yardstick, and it says Marvel is 8.6× as clustered as chance. But that baseline has no hubs, and hubs create triangles on their own: Spider-Man's 106 friends form 5,565 pairs, and some will be linked by sheer volume. Against a random network we can't tell those triangles from real structure. The choice of null model changes the conclusion, not just the number.

The right baseline keeps every character's degree and scrambles everything else. There are two ways to build it:

  • Configuration model: give each character \(k_i\) "stubs" and pair the stubs up at random. This creates self-loops and double links, and deleting them trims the hubs.
  • Degree-preserving edge swap: take two links A–B and C–D and rewire them to A–D and C–B, ten times per link. Every degree stays exact, so this is the one we use.

Shuffle 50 times and every property gets a distribution for "nothing interesting going on". Then score Marvel against it:

\(z = \dfrac{x_{\text{Marvel}} - \mu_{\text{shuffles}}}{\sigma_{\text{shuffles}}}\)z-score: how many standard deviations Marvel sits from the shuffles' mean.
\(p = \dfrac{1 + \#\,\text{shuffles as extreme}}{1 + 50}\)Empirical p-value: the share of shuffles at least as far from their mean as Marvel, in either direction. The +1 counts Marvel itself, so \(p\) is never 0.

If a property survives the shuffle, the degrees explain it; if it dies, it lives in who links to whom. The friendship paradox survives: its formula needs nothing but the degrees. Clustering dies. The shuffle's hubs do make triangles (0.155, about 4× a random network), but they account for only 42% of Marvel's extra clustering.

Putting it together

Marvel is a small world with a heavy tail, and its degrees do most of the work. They make the hubs and the friendship paradox: keep the degrees, scramble the rest, and both are still there. The short paths come even cheaper, from the sheer number of links.

What the degrees can't make is the other 58% of the triangles. That lives in the wiring: characters whose articles mention each other, probably because they share a team or a storyline. No model on this page produces it, and finding those groups is what week 4's communities are for.

The friendship paradox is in the degrees. The triangles are in the wiring.

What surprised us

  • Chance nails six degrees. Short paths are the famous property of real networks, yet a random network matches Marvel's distance to within 0.01 hops.
  • The small-world model has almost no friendship paradox (1.02). It gets Marvel's clustering and distance but gives everyone the same ten neighbours, so its degrees barely vary.

Choices we made

Each could have gone the other way and changed a number above.

  • Undirected giant component (277 of 303) Distance needs a connected network, and all the models are undirected. A random network this dense is far past \(\langle k\rangle = 1\) (a giant component) and \(\ln n = 5.6\) (no isolates), expecting \(n e^{-\langle k\rangle} = 0.01\) isolated nodes. So only Marvel has outsiders to leave out: week 1's isolates and island, 26 characters.
  • Watts–Strogatz rewiring q = 0.2 The small-world range leaves clustering at 0.49 or more; q = 0.2 matches Marvel. So this model's clustering is fitted, not predicted.
  • 5 links per Barabási–Albert newcomer 1,360 links, 4% short of Marvel. 6 would overshoot by 14%.
  • Edge swaps, not the configuration model Swaps keep every degree exactly. The configuration model's clean-up removes links mostly from the hubs, so its degrees stop being Marvel's.
  • Exact averages, not samples The friendship paradox and friend chances are computed over every character, so there's no sampling noise.
  • 50 runs, two-sided test, p < 0.05 Set before looking. With 50 shuffles the smallest possible p is 0.02, so the z-score shows the strength.

What we can't conclude

  • That Marvel grew by preferential attachment. The model can make hubs like Marvel's; that doesn't show it did.
  • That the degrees follow a power law. The tail is heavy, but week 1's test favoured an exponential.
  • Why the triangles are there. The shuffle rules out the degrees, nothing more. Teams and shared storylines are our guess, and a question for week 4.
