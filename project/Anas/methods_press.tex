\textbf{Press releases.} We collected the press releases that House members posted on their official house.gov sites, following each site's press-release listing, between 3 January 2025 and 20 November 2026, the day of the download: 55,023 releases from 412 members, each collected back to the start of the window or the end of their listing; 41 release pages failed to load or had no text. Not collected: four members whose listing loads only with JavaScript or was gone, Jim Jordan (listing ends in 2024), and the 12 who had left office. Three texts posted by five or more members (64 releases) were set aside as templates, and sentences a member repeats across releases (84, in 41 members) were not counted as that release's content.

\textbf{Names and bills in releases.} Releases were matched with the floor's matcher plus a press layer that adds, among other rules, full names without a title, the first names members use in their own releases, and a member's party--state tag (``R-NY''). On 40 releases drawn without the matcher (30 containing another member's surname, 10 without), five blind AI readers (Claude) listed every House member named and agreed on all 77; one author read the seven entries flagged for a human look and agreed with the readers in each. The matcher found 74 (recall 0.96, precision 0.95); a middle-initial rule added after this check brought it to 75 (0.97 and 0.96), measured on the same sample. A release is linked to a House bill of the 119th Congress by its number or by an official title that belongs to that bill alone (30 of 30 correct for each, three blind AI readers, unanimous). A co-lead is the bill's sponsor or a non-withdrawn original cosponsor; party leaders are the seven in the House leadership list.

\textbf{Exposure and tests.} District lean is the 2025 Cook PVI of the district a member served (Wikipedia revision of 25 May 2025), which agrees with the 2024 presidential margin ($r = 0.999$); in the nine states that redrew for 2026, we keep the served district because releases were written for its voters. Exposure gaps are tested by shuffling the exposed label among the members of each party (10,000 shuffles, two-sided). The home map ties two members when one names the other (co-leads included, since a named partner is a tie); its exposed--safe comparisons use the same shuffle, with Holm's correction over the ten comparisons \cite{holm1979}, which keeps the chance of any false positive among them at 5\% without assuming the tests are independent; and ties between exposed members are compared with 1,000 rewirings that keep every member's number of cross-party ties.

\textbf{Two checks on the main result.} Both were designed and written down before they were run. \emph{What the names do:} 60 of the counted names, 15 per party and seat, drawn by member first and then by release (seed 2805), were coded by three blind AI readers who did not see the seat, as partner or praise, criticism, neutral mention, or wrong match; a release that both works with and criticises the named member counts as criticism. They agreed on 56; one author read the four others. \emph{Ideology:} each member's first DW-NOMINATE dimension (Voteview), signed so that higher is further from the centre in their own party's direction, ranks members into five bands within each party (three as a check). The exposed label is shuffled only within party and band (10,000 shuffles); the mean of these shuffles is the gap ideology alone would give.

\textbf{Bipartisan releases.} A release says ``bipartisan'' when the word appears outside the member's repeated sentences; it has a partner when it names a House member of the other party (not a party leader) or cites a bill with one as co-lead. Three blind AI readers checked 30 releases with a named partner and 30 without one; one author read the two disagreements and the two close calls that decided the threshold fixed beforehand.
