## Westwood's year: 2022

Westwood, S. J. "The Partisanship of Bipartisanship: How Representatives Use Bipartisan Assertions to
Cultivate Support." *Political Behavior* 44(3), 1411–1435. DOI 10.1007/s11109-020-09659-6.

The article went online on 2 January 2021 and was printed in the September 2022 issue. Both years in the
archive are therefore explicable. The citation is **Westwood (2022)**, the year of the volume, which is
also how the author's own research lab cites it.

## The two the plan asks for

**Westwood (2022).** *Source: the abstract and introduction on the publisher's page, read 18 November 2026
through DTU's library access.*

- Uses 434,266 House floor speeches, 1994 to 2018, matched to the final floor votes on the bills discussed.
- Members of every ideological position call their work bipartisan about equally often, and a member's
  bipartisan rhetoric is unrelated to their bipartisan action. Most bipartisan claims are made about bills
  that win only trivial support from the other party. Marginal members, who need opposition voters, make
  the most bipartisan appeals.
- In experiments, calling a bill bipartisan raised support and lowered its perceived extremity even when
  the bill was overtly partisan. Adding a single member of the other party and the label moved opinion more
  than a large real cross-party coalition did.

*For us:* the closest study. It shows that the bipartisan **word** on the floor says little about the
votes behind it. We look at **named partners** and check each against the cosponsorship record. Westwood's
text is floor speech, like our floor map.

**Harbridge (2015).** *Is Bipartisanship Dead? Policy Agreement and Agenda-Setting in the House of
Representatives.* Cambridge University Press. DOI 10.1017/CBO9781139942324. *Source: publisher's abstract.*

- Between 1973 and 2004 bipartisan agreement fell in roll-call votes but stayed much more stable in bill
  cosponsorship coalitions.
- The difference comes from agenda-setting: House leaders moved from bringing bills with bipartisan
  agreement to the floor in the 1970s to bringing bills with partisan disagreement by the 1990s.
- So how divided the House looks depends on which record of its behaviour one reads.

*For us:* the precedent for comparing two records of the same chamber and finding different levels of
division. Harbridge compares votes with cosponsorship. We compare cosponsorship with speech.

## Further references

**Fowler (2006).** "Connecting the Congress: A Study of Cosponsorship Networks." *Political Analysis*
14(4), 456–487. DOI 10.1093/pan/mpl002. *Abstract.* Builds the cosponsorship network of 280,000 bills,
1973 to 2004, with a directed tie from each cosponsor to the sponsor, and shows that a member's position
in it predicts legislative influence and vote choice beyond party and ideology. *For us:* the definition
of the working tie. Our working map is Fowler's network made undirected.

**Neal (2020).** "A sign of the times? Weak and strong polarization in the U.S. Congress, 1973–2016."
*Social Networks* 60, 103–112. DOI 10.1016/j.socnet.2018.07.007. *Abstract.* Infers signed networks from
cosponsorship with a statistical backbone model and separates weak polarization (ties between the parties
are absent) from strong polarization (ties between them are negative). Finds both in both chambers, both
rising, both organised by party. *For us:* two things. Thinning a dense cosponsorship network with a
statistical backbone is established practice. And the weak or strong distinction is exactly what our
hand-labelling addresses: a cross-party mention can be a positive tie or a negative one.

**Green, Shoub, Blum and Cormack (2024).** "Cross-Platform Partisan Positioning in Congressional Speech."
*Political Research Quarterly* 77(3), 653–668. DOI 10.1177/10659129241236685. *Abstract.* Estimates a
partisan dimension in tweets, Facebook posts, e-newsletters, press releases and one-minute floor speeches
of the 116th Congress. The parties are separable everywhere, but venues with national audiences are more
polarized than venues with local ones, and the authors conclude that measured polarization depends on
where one looks. *For us:* the nearest thing to our question in the text literature, done with vocabulary
and no network. It motivates the close-up, since press releases and the floor have different audiences.

**Gentzkow, Shapiro and Taddy (2019).** "Measuring Group Differences in High-Dimensional Choices: Method
and Application to Congressional Speech." *Econometrica* 87(4), 1307–1340. DOI 10.3982/ECTA16566.
*Abstract.* Defines the partisanship of speech as how easily a listener could infer a speaker's party from
one utterance, and finds it low and flat for a century, then rising sharply from the early 1990s. *For
us:* the standard result that members of the two parties **sound** different. We ask whom they name, a
different property of the same speeches.

**Grimmer (2013).** *Representational Style in Congress: What Legislators Say and Why It Matters.*
Cambridge University Press. DOI 10.1017/CBO9781139207782. *Publisher's abstract.* Uses a large collection
of legislators' texts to measure how they present their work to constituents, and argues that these
presentations are strategic and shape how constituents judge them. *For us:* the reference for treating
press releases as a deliberate presentation to voters, which is what the close-up assumes. The abstract
does not name the text source. The book is known for Senate press releases, but that needs checking in
the book before the paper says so.

**Harbridge and Malhotra (2011).** "Electoral Incentives and Partisan Conflict in Congress: Evidence from
Survey Experiments." *American Journal of Political Science* 55(3), 494–510.
DOI 10.1111/j.1540-5907.2011.00517.x. *Abstract.* Independents and weak partisans support members with a
bipartisan image more, strong partisans support them less. *For us:* why a member in an exposed seat has a
reason to show cross-party partners and a member in a safe seat does not. One sentence in the
Introduction, and the reason the press-release sample is spread over district lean.

## Considered and left out

- Andris et al. (2015), *PLOS ONE* 10(4), e0123507: rising non-cooperation in House roll-call agreement
  networks, 1949 to 2012. Real and relevant, but it repeats the point Neal makes and the page limit is six.
- Grimmer, Messing and Westwood (2012), *APSR* 106(4), 703–719: credit-claiming messages. About spending,
  not partners.
- Kirkland (2011), *Journal of Politics* 73(3), 887–898: weak ties and legislative success. Tangential.
- Mayhew (1974), *Congress: The Electoral Connection*, Yale University Press: the classic source for

## Is the comparison new?

The README marks as unverified the claim that nobody has compared named colleagues with cosponsorship
ties. One further search on 14 November 2026 for networks of members naming each other in floor speeches
found none: the results were vocabulary studies of the Record and a study of Twitter mention networks
among members. Two short search sessions do not establish absence. The paper should say "we are not aware
of" and no more.[^novel]
