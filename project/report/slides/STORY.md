# The story of the three slides

One paragraph per slide, about 60 seconds each, one speaker per slide (DECISIONS G25). Numbers are from `main2.tex`,
except Bacon's own numbers, which come from the files named in brackets. Don Bacon (R, Nebraska) is the member we
follow through the talk: he is ringed on slide 1, quoted on slide 2 and a labelled hub on slide 3.

**Slide 1 (Martin). They vote in two camps. Do they also name each other in two camps?** House members vote apart
and they talk apart. So when one of them names a colleague from the other party on the floor, you'd expect an
attack. We took the same 450 members and drew them twice. In the working map, two members are tied when one
cosponsors the other's bill. In the floor map, they are tied when one names the other in a floor speech. Look at
the purple: those are ties across the aisle. On the floor, 38.7% of ties cross. In the working map, cut to the same
size, 28.6% do. Watch Don Bacon, a Nebraska Republican, sitting right where the two parties meet. The House is less
divided in whom it names than in whom it works with.

**Slide 2 (Diego). You'd expect attacks. Most of the time, it's a thank-you.** We read 100 mentions by hand. Most
cross-party mentions are thanks and credit (69%). Bacon, for one, thanks Democrat Ro Khanna "for his collaboration
this year" [Diego/d2_labelling, mention 58]. Only 7% disagree. Some of the mixing is built into how the House runs.
Each debate puts a manager from each party on the floor, and that explains about two thirds of the floor map's
division. When an algorithm looks for groups in the floor map, it doesn't find the parties. It finds committees:
Appropriations, Ways and Means. Naming across the aisle is committee work done in public.

**Slide 3 (Anas). At home, members in close races name the other party most.** Then we followed the members home, to
55,023 press releases on their own websites. The members who name the other party most are the ones whose seat is
at risk, in districts that lean their way by five points or less. In the closest Republican seats, about one release
in five names a Democrat, and usually as a partner. Bacon's district leans three points to the Democrats, and 53 of
his 163 releases name one [Anas/a4/out/member_shares.csv]. On the map of who names whom at home, members like him
sit where the two parties meet. We can't tell whether the seat makes them do it or whether they're simply the
moderates, because in our data they're mostly the same people. What we can say is that how divided Congress looks
depends on which record you read. And a name, unlike the word "bipartisan", points at a person, so it can be
checked.
